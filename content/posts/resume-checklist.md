# Resume Checklist: 10 Things to Verify Before Submitting

**WordPress Post Settings:**
- **Category:** Guides & Templates
- **Tags:** resume, checklist, entry-level
- **Excerpt:** A step-by-step checklist to make sure your resume is complete, consistent, and ready to send. Covers formatting, content, and the small details that make a big difference.

---

Before you send your resume to a potential employer, run through this checklist. These ten items cover the most common mistakes that entry-level candidates make — and each one is easy to fix.

## 1. Contact Information Is Complete and Professional

Your resume should include:
- **Full name** — the same name you use professionally
- **Phone number** — one reliable number where you can be reached
- **Email address** — use a professional-sounding email (firstname.lastname@email.com), not a casual handle
- **Location** — city and state/region is enough; a full street address is unnecessary
- **LinkedIn profile** (optional) — include it only if your profile is updated and complete

**What to skip:** Date of birth, photo (unless culturally expected in your region), marital status.

## 2. The File Is Named Properly

Name your resume file clearly:

✅ `Priya_Sharma_Resume.pdf`  
✅ `JohnDoe_Resume_2026.pdf`  
❌ `resume_final_v3_FINAL.docx`  
❌ `Document1.pdf`

Always submit as **PDF** unless the employer specifically requests a different format. PDFs preserve your formatting across different devices.

## 3. Formatting Is Clean and Consistent

Check these formatting basics:
- [ ] One font for the entire document (Inter, Calibri, or Arial work well)
- [ ] Font size between 10pt and 12pt for body text
- [ ] Headings are clearly distinguishable (bold, slightly larger, or capitalized)
- [ ] Margins are consistent (0.5" to 1" on all sides)
- [ ] Bullet points use the same style throughout
- [ ] Dates are formatted consistently (e.g., always "Jan 2025" or "January 2025", not a mix)
- [ ] No orphaned headings at the bottom of a page

## 4. It Fits on One Page (Usually)

For students and fresh graduates, a **one-page resume** is standard. If you're exceeding one page, ask yourself:
- Is every item directly relevant to the role I'm applying for?
- Can any descriptions be shortened without losing meaning?
- Are there sections I can remove entirely (e.g., an objective statement you don't need)?

**Exception:** If you have extensive research, publications, or project work that's directly relevant, a second page is acceptable — but make sure page one is strong enough to stand alone.

## 5. There Are No Spelling or Grammar Errors

This sounds obvious, but it remains the most common resume mistake:
- Run spellcheck, but don't rely on it alone
- Read your resume out loud — you'll catch awkward phrasing
- Ask someone else to proofread it (a friend, classmate, or mentor)
- Pay special attention to **company names** and **technical terms** — spellcheck won't catch "Amazone" or "Pythn"

## 6. Action Verbs Lead Every Bullet Point

Each bullet point under your experience or projects should start with a **strong action verb**:

✅ "Developed a Python script to automate data entry, reducing manual work by 40%"  
✅ "Organized a campus workshop on resume writing for 50+ students"  
❌ "Was responsible for managing social media"  
❌ "Helped with various tasks in the marketing department"

**Useful action verbs for entry-level resumes:** Designed, Built, Created, Organized, Analyzed, Implemented, Researched, Coordinated, Presented, Contributed.

## 7. Accomplishments Include Numbers Where Possible

Quantify your impact wherever you can:
- "Managed a team of **5** volunteers"
- "Increased social media engagement by **25%** over 3 months"
- "Completed **12** client projects during internship"
- "Built a web application used by **200+** students"

Even rough numbers are better than vague descriptions. If you can't quantify, at least be specific about what you did and what the result was.

## 8. Education Section Is Accurate

For students and recent graduates, Education usually goes near the top. Include:
- **Degree name** and **major/specialization**
- **University name** and **expected/actual graduation date**
- **GPA** — include if it's above 3.0/4.0 (or equivalent). If it's lower, it's often better to leave it out.
- **Relevant coursework** — list 3–5 courses only if they're directly relevant to the role
- **Academic honors** — Dean's list, scholarships, or awards

## 9. Skills Section Is Honest and Relevant

List only skills you can actually demonstrate or discuss in an interview:

✅ Programming languages you've used in projects  
✅ Tools you've worked with (Excel, Figma, Git)  
✅ Certifications you've completed  
❌ Skills you've only read about but never practiced  
❌ Vague skills like "hard worker" or "team player" (these belong in your cover letter or interview, not your skills list)

**Organize skills by category** if you have several: Programming Languages, Tools & Platforms, Languages, Certifications.

## 10. It's Tailored to the Specific Role

This is the step most candidates skip — and it makes the biggest difference:
- [ ] Read the job description carefully
- [ ] Match your resume's language to the keywords and requirements in the posting
- [ ] Reorder your bullet points so the most relevant ones come first
- [ ] Adjust your professional summary (if you have one) to address what this specific employer needs

You don't need a completely different resume for every application, but you should adjust the emphasis and language for each role.

---

## Printable Checklist Summary

Use this as a quick reference before each submission:

- [ ] Contact info is complete and professional
- [ ] File is named properly (FirstName_LastName_Resume.pdf)
- [ ] Formatting is clean and consistent
- [ ] Fits on one page (for entry-level)
- [ ] No spelling or grammar errors
- [ ] Bullet points start with action verbs
- [ ] Accomplishments include numbers
- [ ] Education section is accurate
- [ ] Skills section is honest
- [ ] Resume is tailored to the specific role
